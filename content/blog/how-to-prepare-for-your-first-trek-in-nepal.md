So you have decided to trek in [Nepal.](https://waveuphimalayas.com/blog/where-is-nepal-located/) Maybe you have seen the photographs of Poon Hill at sunrise, or a friend came back from Langtang Valley talking about teahouse dal bhat and rhododendron forests. Now the questions begin: Which trek? When should I go? Do I need a guide? How fit do I need to be? What do I pack?

This guide is written for you — **the first-time trekker** who wants honest answers, not marketing promises. Below is everything you need to know to prepare for your first trek in Nepal in 2026 and 2027, from choosing a route to lacing up your boots.

## Start With the Right Trek, Not the Most Famous One

The most common first-timer mistake is booking Everest Base Camp because it is the name everyone knows. EBC is a superb goal, but it is a demanding trek at serious altitude, and it is far from the only way to experience the Himalaya. A good first trek builds your confidence and shows you how your body reacts to height, so you can aim higher next time.

**Ghorepani Poon Hill** is the best all-round first trek in Nepal for most beginners. It is short, teahouse-based and tops out at about 3,210 m, with sunrise views over the Annapurna and Dhaulagiri ranges. A typical itinerary needs four to five walking days from the Pokhara side, which suits travellers with limited time. Do not mistake "beginner-friendly" for "easy": the route has steep climbs and thousands of stone steps, so train before you go.

**Langtang Valley** is a great second step, or a first trek for people who want more time in the mountains. The trail climbs through forest and Tamang villages into the wide upper valley around Kyanjin Gompa. It starts by road from Kathmandu, so there is no mountain flight to worry about, and at a relaxed pace it takes seven to nine walking days.

**Annapurna Base Camp (ABC)** suits fit beginners. It reaches 4,130 m and delivers scenery that rivals Everest in fewer days than EBC. The trade-off is a longer trek and more altitude than Poon Hill.

| Trek | Walking days | Highest point | Best for | What to know |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ghorepani Poon Hill | 4–5 | about 3,210 m | The best first trek overall | Short and accessible, but expect lots of steps |
| Langtang Valley | 7–9 | about 3,800–3,900 m (Kyanjin area) | A fuller journey with fewer crowds | Longer days and more altitude, so do not rush |
| Annapurna Base Camp | 7–10 | 4,130 m | Fit beginners with more time | Big mountain scenery in fewer days than EBC |
| Everest Base Camp | 12–14 | about 5,364 m | Experienced or well-acclimatized trekkers | Best saved for a second or third trek |

**How many days do you need?** A short beginner trek like Poon Hill fits into roughly 7–9 days door to door, counting your Kathmandu arrival, transport and a buffer day. Langtang and ABC usually take 10–12 days as complete itineraries. If you only have one week, Poon Hill is the closest match. ## Timing: The Best Time to Trek in Nepal

Nepal has two peak trekking seasons, and both work well for beginners.

**Autumn (September to November)** is the favourite. The monsoon has washed the air clean, trails are dry and mountain views are at their sharpest. October is often the single best month, with the clearest skies, and it is also the busiest, so teahouses on popular trails fill fast. Late September and November are quieter and still excellent.

**Spring (March to May)** is the second peak season, and the season of colour. Rhododendrons bloom across the mid-hills, temperatures are milder and days are longer. Mornings are usually clear, though afternoon haze or cloud is common, especially near Everest and Manaslu.

**March, April, October and November** are the sweet spots. March opens spring trekking, April is peak rhododendron season, October has the best views, and November offers stable weather with fewer people than October.

**Winter (December to February)** suits low-altitude routes such as Poon Hill or the lower Langtang valley, but nights are cold, high passes can close and some lodges shut. **Monsoon (June to August)** brings heavy rain, muddy trails, landslides and leeches on lower trails, so we do not recommend it for a first trek.

**Spring or autumn?** Choose spring for warmer weather and wildflowers, and autumn for the crispest, most reliable views. Both are busy on popular routes, so book your trek and domestic flights early. Planning for autumn 2026 or spring 2027? It is mid-November 2026 as we write this, so the main autumn window is closing and spring 2027 (March to May) is the best target for most first-timers: you have time to train, book and sort your gear without rushing. Autumn 2027 (September to November) is the next big window if you want the clearest views.

## Permits, Guides and Costs: What to Know for 2026 and 2027

This is the section where outdated blog posts cause the most confusion. Nepal changed its trekking rules in 2023 and adjusted parts of them again in 2026. Here is the current picture for the 2026 and 2027 seasons, though fees and enforcement change (the figures below are 2026 rates and may be updated for 2027), so we confirm everything for your exact route before you pay.

### Do you need a guide to trek in Nepal?

For most main trekking regions, yes. Under a rule in force since 31 March 2023, foreign trekkers on the Nepal Tourism Board's listed routes need a licensed guide and a TIMS card issued through a registered trekking agency. Routes that cross a national park or conservation area generally fall under this rule. Some online posts claim the guide requirement has been lifted. Official enforcement is what counts at checkpoints, and a guide is the safest way to avoid problems, so every WaveUp Himalayas trek includes a licensed guide.

In March 2026 Nepal also eased one part of the restricted-area rules: a single traveller can now obtain a restricted-area permit without a second person in the group. A licensed guide and a registered agency are still required there, and one guide may lead up to seven trekkers.

### Which permits will you need?

Most popular regions need a park or conservation area permit plus a TIMS card. Typical 2026 fees for foreign trekkers:

| Permit | Fee (foreign trekkers) | Where it applies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Annapurna Conservation Area (ACAP) | NPR 3,000 (about US$19) | Poon Hill, Annapurna Base Camp, Mardi Himal, Annapurna Circuit |
| Langtang National Park | NPR 3,000 (about US$19) | Langtang Valley |
| Sagarmatha National Park | NPR 3,000 (about US$19) | Everest region |
| Khumbu local municipality fee | NPR 3,000 (about US$19) | Everest region, paid in addition to the park fee |
| TIMS card | about NPR 1,000–2,000 | Most trekking regions; the price depends on how it is issued, and it is now handled through registered agencies |

Restricted areas such as Upper Mustang, Manaslu, Tsum Valley, Nar Phu, Upper Dolpo and Humla need extra special permits that only an agency can arrange. When you book with us, we handle every permit, so you do not have to chase paperwork in Kathmandu or Pokhara.

### What does a trek in Nepal cost?

The total depends on your route, group size, comfort level and season. Peak-season months (October–November and March–April) run roughly 10–15% higher than the off-season. As a rough guide, typical daily rates for services are:

| Service | Approximate daily rate (2026) |
| --- | --- |
| Licensed English-speaking guide | US$30–50 |
| Porter-guide (carries about 15 kg and guides) | US$25–35 |
| Porter only (carries about 20 kg) | US$20–30 |

A guide's daily fee normally covers their own food, lodging and insurance. Budget separately for tips, commonly around US$100–200 for a guide and US$60–120 for a porter on a standard trek, plus domestic flights or transfers, teahouse meals, hot showers and device charging.

### Do you need travel insurance?

Yes, and it must cover trekking at altitude. Many standard policies exclude trekking or stop at 3,000 m. Check that yours covers your route's highest point and includes emergency helicopter evacuation. Reputable agencies ask for proof of insurance before departure, and it is also part of restricted-area permit applications. Read the small print rather than relying on a generic policy.

## Fitness and Health: How Fit Do You Really Need to Be?

You do not need to be an athlete to trek in Nepal, but you do need to prepare. A beginner trek like Poon Hill means five or six hours of walking over uneven ground on most days, with a lot of stair climbing. Langtang and Annapurna Base Camp ask for more sustained effort.

### The 8-week Nepal trek fitness training plan

If you start from a reasonable base of activity, eight weeks is enough to build trek-specific fitness. Start earlier (8–12 weeks out) if you are less active.

| Weeks | Focus | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1–2 | Build the habit | Walk 45–60 minutes on four days a week, ideally on hills or stairs. Add two short strength sessions (squats, lunges, step-ups, core). |
| 3–4 | Add load and duration | Extend one walk to 2–3 hours with a 5 kg daypack. Increase stair or hill repeats. Keep two strength sessions. |
| 5–6 | Simulate trekking days | One 4–5 hour walk a week with a 7–10 kg pack, preferably on uneven trails. Add single-leg strength and balance work. |
| 7–8 | Taper and test | Do your longest walk (5–6 hours, loaded) early in week 7. In week 8 cut volume but keep moving daily. Practise with your poles and boots. |

The most useful single training tool is stairs. Nepal's trails climb and drop constantly, and stone staircases define routes like Poon Hill. If you can train on a stairwell or stadium steps, do it. Break in your boots long before you fly, and practise using trekking poles on descents, where most knee strain happens.

### Altitude sickness: how to avoid it

Acute mountain sickness (AMS) can affect anyone regardless of fitness. The risk rises above roughly 3,000 m, and it grows quickly if you ascend fast. The golden rules are simple:

- **Walk high, sleep low.** Take acclimatization days seriously and never rush the ascent.
- **Keep sleeping-altitude gains modest** once above 3,000 m. A common guideline is no more than 400–500 m of sleeping elevation gain per day, with a rest day roughly every 1,000 m.
- **Hydrate, eat well and avoid alcohol** while gaining height.
- **Know the symptoms:** headache, nausea, dizziness, unusual fatigue and poor sleep. If they do not ease with rest, stop climbing. If they worsen, descend. Going down is the most reliable treatment.
- **Ask a doctor about Diamox** (acetazolamide) before you travel. It helps some trekkers prevent AMS, but suitability and dose should be confirmed by a clinician, and it never replaces proper acclimatization.

A good operator builds rest days into your itinerary and briefs you on AMS before you start walking. Our guides check in with every trekker each day.

## Nepal Trekking Packing List: What to Bring, Rent and Leave Behind

The biggest mistake first-time trekkers make is overpacking. A heavy bag makes every step harder, and the people who enjoy themselves most are usually the ones who packed lightest. As a target, aim for about 7–10 kg in your daypack and 10–12 kg in the duffel your porter carries, and keep your total under any flight weight limit, which is commonly around 15 kg on mountain flights.

### The layering system

Four layers handle everything from a warm lower valley to a freezing high camp:

- **Base layer:** moisture-wicking synthetic or merino. Bring two sets.
- **Fleece or mid-layer:** for warmth on the move and in the teahouse.
- **Down jacket:** a warm, packable jacket for mornings, evenings and rest stops.
- **Waterproof shell:** jacket and trousers. This is not optional.

### Essential gear checklist

- Broken-in waterproof trekking boots. Bring these from home and never arrive with new boots.
- Sleeping bag rated to about −10°C to −15°C for most teahouse treks. You can rent one in Kathmandu, but check the rating and cleanliness first.
- Adjustable trekking poles
- Water bottle plus purification tablets, a filter or a UV purifier
- Headlamp with spare batteries
- High-SPF sunscreen, lip balm, sunglasses and a brimmed hat
- Power bank of 10,000 mAh or more
- First-aid kit with blister care, painkillers, rehydration salts and any personal medication
- Toilet paper and hand sanitiser (carry your own, and do not flush paper)
- Earplugs and an eye mask, because teahouse walls are thin
- Passport copies, permits, insurance details and cash in Nepali rupees

### Rent or buy in Kathmandu?

Thamel is full of gear shops, and many items cost far less there than at home, though quality varies. Bring your own **boots and waterproof shell**, because fit and reliability matter most. Rent or buy bulky items such as down jackets, sleeping bags and poles in Kathmandu.

**What to leave behind:** a second pair of heavy shoes (camp sandals are enough), "just in case" clothing, and anything you hesitate over. A good test: take everything out, then put back only what you would be happy to carry up five flights of stairs three times in a row.

## What to Expect From a Nepal Teahouse Trek

Teahouse trekking is what makes Nepal special. Instead of carrying tents and stoves, you walk from village to village and stay in family-run lodges, eating hot meals and sleeping indoors every night.

### Rooms and facilities

Rooms are simple, usually twin-share, with foam mattresses, a pillow and a blanket. Walls are thin, bedrooms are unheated and bathrooms are often shared, especially as you gain altitude. Standards are highest on the Annapurna and Everest trails, where some lodges offer attached bathrooms and solar-heated showers. Quieter routes are more basic. Bring your own sleeping bag, because lodge blankets are an extra layer rather than a substitute.

### Food and teahouse etiquette

Menus look long, but the trekker's staple is **dal bhat**: lentil soup with rice and vegetable sides, usually with free refills. Lodges expect you to eat dinner and breakfast where you sleep, since room rates are kept low on that understanding. Vegetarian food is the safest choice at altitude, and garlic soup and ginger-lemon-honey tea are trail favourites.

A few cultural basics go a long way: greet people with "namaste", remove your boots before entering rooms and dining areas, dress modestly in villages, ask before photographing people, and keep noise down in the evening. Walk clockwise around mani walls and stupas.

### A typical trekking day

Wake-up is around 6:00–6:30 a.m. You walk for roughly five to seven hours with breaks and reach the next teahouse in the afternoon. Evenings are spent in the communal dining room around the stove, often the only heated space in the building, eating, drying socks and swapping stories with trekkers from around the world.

## First-Time Trekker Mistakes to Avoid

Even well-prepared beginners make avoidable errors. These are the ones we see most often:

- **Rushing the ascent.** The mountain does not care how fit you are, only how well you acclimatize. Build rest days into the plan and stick to them.
- **Overpacking.** A heavy pack makes every step harder. Pack light and trust that teahouses stock the basics.
- **Skipping fitness preparation.** Poon Hill is beginner-friendly, but it still means steep stone steps. Train first.
- **Choosing the wrong season.** Monsoon rain and landslide risk are real, and winter cold closes high routes. Trek in spring or autumn if you can.
- **Underestimating the cold.** Teahouse bedrooms are unheated even in peak season. A proper sleeping bag and warm layers are essential.
- **Buying the wrong insurance.** Standard policies often exclude trekking. Check the altitude limit and evacuation cover before you leave home.
- **Wearing new boots.** Blisters end more first treks than bad weather. Break your boots in for weeks.
- **Forgetting buffer days.** Mountain flights are often delayed by weather, so leave a spare day in Kathmandu or Pokhara.
- **Ignoring teahouse etiquette.** Eating where you sleep, asking before taking photos and keeping noise down all help you and the people who host you.

## Tips for Women and Solo Travellers

Nepal is generally a welcoming and safe destination for women and solo trekkers, and a few practical points make it easier.

- **Female guides are available.** They are booked early in spring, so ask early if you prefer one.
- **Dress modestly in villages.** A scarf or buff covers shoulders, and avoid revealing clothing away from the trail.
- **Solo does not mean alone.** You can travel solo, but under current rules you still trek with a licensed guide through a registered agency, which is as much a safety net as a regulation. Joining a small group is a good way to meet people and share costs.
- **Shared rooms and bathrooms are the norm.** A sleeping bag liner adds hygiene and warmth, and earplugs help with thin walls.
- **Menstrual supplies:** pack enough for the whole trek plus a small bag for used items, because availability above the road head is limited and waste must be carried out.
- **Tell someone your plan.** Share your itinerary and insurance details with a contact at home.
