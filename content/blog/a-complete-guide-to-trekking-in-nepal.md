## Best Time to Trek in Nepal

The **best time to trek in Nepal** depends on where you go, what you want to see, and how you feel about crowds. Nepal has four main trekking seasons—autumn, spring, winter, and monsoon—and each one offers a different experience. For most trekkers, **autumn (October–November)** and **spring (March–May)** are the best windows, but winter and monsoon can also work for select routes.

**Autumn: The Peak Season**

![](/images/blog/annapurna-poonhill-viewtreks.webp "Annapurna Base Camp in Autumn")

October and November are the most popular months for **Nepal trekking**. The monsoon has cleared, the air is crisp, and the mountain views are spectacular. This is the best time for the **Everest Base Camp trek**, **Annapurna Circuit trek**, **Manaslu Circuit trek**, and most high-altitude routes. Trails are lively, teahouses are full, and the weather is generally stable. The trade-off is higher prices and more crowds, so book early.

**Spring: The Second Peak**

March to May is another excellent window for **Himalayan trekking in Nepal**. Temperatures are warmer, rhododendron forests are in full bloom, and the trails are colorful and lively. April and May are especially popular for the **Everest Base Camp trek** and **Annapurna Base Camp trek**. Lower elevations can be hazy, and Everest region trails get busy, but the overall experience is beautiful.

**Winter: Quiet and Cold**

December to February offers peaceful trails, lower teahouse rates, and clear mountain views. However, high passes like **Thorong La** on the **Annapurna Circuit** can be dangerous, and many lodges above 4,000 meters close for the season. Lower-altitude treks such as the **Ghorepani Poon Hill trek** and **Mardi Himal trek** remain possible and rewarding.

**Monsoon: For Select Regions**

June to September is generally not recommended for high-altitude trekking. Rain, clouds, and slippery trails make travel difficult, and flights to Lukla are often delayed. The exception is the **Upper Mustang trek**, which lies in the rain shadow of the Annapurnas and remains trekkable during monsoon. It is a great choice for travelers who want a unique landscape and fewer crowds.

## Nepal Trekking Permits and Rules

**Nepal trekking permits** are essential for every foreign trekker, and the rules have changed significantly in recent years. Since April 2023, hiring a **licensed guide** is mandatory for all foreign trekkers, and as of March 2026, solo trekkers can now obtain permits for restricted areas—but only through a registered agency with a licensed guide. One guide can lead a maximum of seven trekkers. Permit rules, fees, and checkpoint requirements change often, so always confirm current details with **Waveup Himalayas** before you travel.

![](/images/blog/70dab322-b11f-4a89-aeea-861ebc77deaa.png)

**The Guide Requirement**

Since April 1, 2023, **guides are officially mandatory** for all foreign trekkers in Nepal. This rule is strictly enforced at checkpoints—if you do not have a licensed guide with their official ID, you will be turned back. Your guide also provides critical safety support, including altitude monitoring and emergency rescue coordination.

**TIMS Card**

The **TIMS card** (Trekkers' Information Management System) registers your itinerary, emergency contacts, and insurance details with authorities. It is still required in regions like **Langtang**, **Manaslu**, and **Upper Mustang**, but it has been phased out at checkpoints in the Everest and Annapurna regions. TIMS costs NPR 2,000 for independent trekkers and NPR 1,000 for group trekkers booked through an agency.

**Everest Region Permits**

For the **Everest Base Camp trek**, you need two permits: the **Sagarmatha National Park entry permit** (NPR 3,000) and the **Khumbu Pasang Lhamu Rural Municipality permit** (NPR 2,000), totaling around NPR 5,000 (\~USD 45). TIMS is not required on this route. Both permits can be obtained in Kathmandu or at checkpoints in Monjo and Lukla.

**Annapurna Region Permits**

For **Annapurna Circuit**, **Annapurna Base Camp**, **Ghorepani Poon Hill**, and **Mardi Himal**, you need the **Annapurna Conservation Area Permit (ACAP)** at NPR 3,000 (\~USD 23). TIMS is no longer enforced at checkpoints in the Annapurna region—ACAP alone is sufficient. ACAP can be purchased in Kathmandu or Pokhara before your trek.

**Restricted Area Permits**

**Upper Mustang** now operates on a **USD 50 per person per day** Restricted Area Permit, replacing the old flat USD 500 fee. You also need ACAP, and a licensed guide is mandatory.

For the **Manaslu Circuit**, the **Restricted Area Permit** costs USD 100 for the first 7 days in peak season (September–November) and USD 75 in off-season, plus USD 15/day beyond the first week. You also need the Manaslu Conservation Area Permit (MCAP) and ACAP. As of March 2026, solo trekkers can hold the RAP alone, though the guide requirement remains.

**Where to Get Permits**

Most permits can be arranged through **Waveup Himalayas** as part of your trek package. If purchasing independently, ACAP and Sagarmatha National Park permits are available at the **Nepal Tourism Board office in Kathmandu** or at regional entry points. TIMS cards are issued through the NTB or TAAN offices. Restricted Area Permits must be processed through a registered agency.

## Nepal Trekking Cost

How much does it cost to trek in Nepal? The honest answer is: it depends. Your **Nepal trekking cost** is shaped by the route you choose, the season you travel, the level of comfort you want, and whether you join a group or go private. A short **Ghorepani Poon Hill trek** can be very affordable, while a 14-day **Everest Base Camp trek** with full support costs more. The good news is that Nepal remains excellent value compared to most mountain destinations.

A 14-day trek from your home country often costs between **USD 2,000 and USD 2,500** including the trek package, meals, visa, insurance, and flights. On the trail, budget treks start around **USD 40–70 per day**, while full-service treks cost more.

**What Affects the Price?**

- **Season** – October–November and March–May are busier and more expensive.

- **Group size** – Private treks cost more than joining a group.

- **Guide and porter ratio** – More support means higher cost.

- International flights

## Best Treks in Nepal

Nepal has more than 50 established **Nepal trekking routes**, from gentle village walks to serious high-altitude expeditions. That means there is no single “best trek” in Nepal—only the best trek for you. Your ideal choice depends on your fitness, your available time, the season, and whether you want iconic views, quiet trails, or a real physical challenge. Here are the **best treks in Nepal**, grouped by difficulty.

### Easy to Moderate Treks

**Ghorepani Poon Hill Trek**

- **Duration:** 3–5 days
- **Max altitude:** 3,210 m
- **Best for:** Beginners, families, sunrise views

The **Ghorepani Poon Hill trek** is the classic introduction to Nepal trekking. The sunrise from Poon Hill over Annapurna and Dhaulagiri is unforgettable, and the trail is achievable for most fitness levels.

**Mardi Himal Trek**

- **Duration:** 5–7 days
- **Max altitude:** 4,500 m
- **Best for:** Quiet trails, Machhapuchhre views

A shorter, less crowded alternative in the Annapurna region. The **Mardi Himal trek** offers beautiful ridge walking and close-up views of Machhapuchhre.

**Langtang Valley Trek**

- **Duration:** 7–10 days
- **Max altitude:** 4,773 m
- **Best for:** Close-to-Kathmandu adventure, Tamang culture

A moderate trek through forests, villages, and yak pastures. The **Langtang Valley trek** is a great choice if you want a quieter Himalayan experience without long domestic flights.

### Demanding Treks

**Everest Base Camp Trek**

- **Duration:** 12–14 days
- **Max altitude:** 5,545 m at Kala Patthar
- **Best for:** Iconic mountain views, Sherpa culture

The **Everest Base Camp trek** is the world’s most famous high-altitude walk. It is demanding but non-technical—the challenge is altitude, not climbing skill. You follow the Dudh Koshi valley through Sherpa villages, cross suspension bridges, and finish beside the Khumbu Icefall.

**Annapurna Circuit Trek**

- **Duration:** 12–18 days
- **Max altitude:** 5,416 m at Thorong La
- **Best for:** Diverse landscapes, high-pass challenge

The **Annapurna Circuit trek** crosses from subtropical valleys to high desert plateau. Thorong La is one of the highest trekking passes in the world, making this one of the most rewarding **Nepal trekking routes**.

**Annapurna Base Camp Trek**

- **Duration:** 7–11 days
- **Max altitude:** 4,130 m
- **Best for:** Sanctuary views, moderate challenge

A rewarding trek into the heart of the Annapurna Sanctuary. The **Annapurna Base Camp trek** combines rice terraces, rhododendron forests, and towering mountain walls.

### Hard to Very Hard Treks

**Manaslu Circuit Trek**

![](/images/blog/manaslu-circuit-trek-all-you-need-to-know1.webp "Manaslu Circuit Trek")

- **Duration:** 14–18 days
- **Max altitude:** 5,106 m at Larkya La
- **Best for:** Remote trails, restricted-area adventure

A stunning circuit around the world’s eighth-highest mountain. The **Manaslu Circuit trek** is quieter and wilder than the Annapurna Circuit, with rich Tibetan-influenced culture.

**Everest Three Passes Trek**

- **Duration:** 17–20 days
- **Max altitude:** 5,535 m at Kongma La
- **Best for:** Experienced trekkers, high passes

A very hard route linking Kongma La, Cho La, and Renjo La. The **Everest Three Passes trek** is one of the most challenging teahouse treks in Nepal.

**Gokyo Lakes Trek**

- **Duration:** 11–13 days
- **Max altitude:** 5,357 m at Gokyo Ri
- **Best for:** Turquoise lakes, Everest views

A beautiful alternative to the standard Everest Base Camp route. The **Gokyo Lakes trek** offers stunning glacial lakes and panoramic views of Everest from Gokyo Ri.

**Upper Mustang Trek**

- **Duration:** 10–14 days
- **Max altitude:** 4,000 m+
- **Best for:** Rain-shadow desert, Tibetan culture

A restricted-area trek with dramatic landscapes and ancient walled cities. The **Upper Mustang trek** is also one of the few routes that remains trekkable during monsoon.

## Food and Water on the Trail

Eating and drinking well on the trail is one of the most important parts of a safe and enjoyable trek. **Teahouse trekking in Nepal** makes it easy to find hot meals and clean drinks, but your choices affect your energy, digestion, and how well you handle altitude.

**Food on the Trail**

![](/images/blog/Food-on-the-Annapurna-Base-Camp-Trek.png "Daal Bhat in Annapurna Base Camp")

**Dal bhat**—rice, lentil soup, and curried vegetables—is the staple **Nepal trekking food**. It is nutritious, filling, and often comes with free refills. You will find it in almost every teahouse, and it is one of the best meals for sustained energy on the trail.

Other common dishes include:

- Thukpa (noodle soup)
- Momos
- Pasta
- Fried rice
- Eggs and toast
- Sherpa stew
- Garlic soup

Breakfast usually includes Tibetan bread, porridge, eggs, pancakes, or muesli. As altitude increases, food prices rise because supplies must be carried or flown in. A meal that costs USD 3 in a lower village may cost USD 8–10 above 4,000 meters.

Eat regularly, even if your appetite drops at altitude. Avoiding heavy, greasy meals can help if you have a sensitive stomach. Vegetarian and vegan options are available, but choices become more limited at higher elevations.

**Water on the Trail**

Do not rely on single-use plastic bottles. They are expensive at altitude and harmful to the mountain environment. Instead:

- Carry a reusable water bottle.
- Use purification tablets or a filter.
- Drink 3–4 liters of water per day at altitude.
- Hot water, tea, and ginger lemon honey are also good for hydration and warmth.

Avoid untreated tap water, stream water, and ice in drinks. At teahouses, you can buy boiled or filtered water, but still treat it if you are unsure.

## Altitude Sickness and Safety

Altitude sickness is the most serious risk on any **Nepal trekking** route above 3,000 meters. It can affect anyone—fit or unfit, young or old. The good news is that with proper **Nepal trekking acclimatization**, awareness, and a sensible itinerary, it is largely preventable. The key is to ascend slowly, never ignore symptoms, and be ready to descend if things get worse.

### Prevention Rules

- Ascend no more than **300–500 meters per day** above 3,000 meters.
- Take a **rest day every three to four days**.
- **Climb high, sleep low**—take short hikes during rest days but return to a lower elevation to sleep.
- Drink **3–4 liters of water** daily; altitude dehydrates you quickly.
- Eat regularly, even if your appetite drops.
- Avoid alcohol and smoking at altitude.
- Never skip the acclimatization days built into your itinerary.

### Symptoms to Watch

**Mild altitude sickness (AMS):**

- Persistent headache
- Nausea or vomiting
- Dizziness
- Fatigue and poor sleep
- Loss of appetite
- Breathlessness with mild effort

**Worsening symptoms:**

- Vomiting that won’t stop
- Confusion or difficulty walking straight
- Breathlessness at rest
- Chest tightness or coughing

**Severe, life-threatening forms:**

- **HACE** (high-altitude cerebral edema) – confusion, hallucinations, loss of coordination
- **HAPE** (high-altitude pulmonary edema) – fluid in the lungs, blue lips, extreme breathlessness

If symptoms worsen, **descend immediately**. Do not wait for morning. Descent is the best and most reliable treatment. **Diamox (Acetazolamide)** can help with acclimatization and reduce symptoms, but it does not replace gradual ascent. Consult your doctor before using it.

### Travel Insurance

Your insurance must cover **high-altitude trekking and helicopter evacuation**. Mountain rescue in Nepal is expensive, and helicopters will not take off without guaranteed payment. Check your policy’s altitude limits, emergency contact number, and evacuation coverage before you travel.

## Nepal Trekking Packing List

Packing smart is one of the most important parts of planning any **Nepal trekking** adventure. The weather in the Himalayas can change quickly—sunny mornings can turn into cold, windy afternoons, and high passes can bring snow even in spring. The golden rule is **layering**: bring moisture-wicking base layers, insulating mid-layers, and a waterproof outer shell. You do not need to overpack, but you do need the right **Nepal trekking gear list** for your route, season, and altitude.

**Clothing**

- Moisture-wicking base layers (top and bottom)
- Fleece or down mid-layer
- Waterproof jacket and pants
- Trekking pants and shorts
- Warm hat and gloves
- Sun hat and buff
- 3–4 pairs of wool trekking socks
- Comfortable t-shirts for lower elevations

**Footwear**

- Broken-in, waterproof hiking boots with ankle support
- Camp shoes or sandals for teahouse evenings
- Gaiters (optional, useful for high passes)

**Gear**

- 30–35L daypack for trail essentials
- 50–60L duffel for porter-carried gear
- 4-season sleeping bag (teahouses can be cold)
- Trekking poles
- Headlamp with spare batteries
- Water purification tablets or filter
- Reusable water bottle
- Power bank and charging cables
- Sunglasses with UV protection
- Sunscreen and lip balm
- Basic first-aid kit with blister treatment
- Earplugs (teahouse walls are thin)
- Toiletries and quick-dry towel

**Documents**

- Passport and multiple photocopies
- Trekking permits and TIMS card (where required)
- Travel insurance details
- Cash in Nepali rupees (ATMs are scarce on trails)
- Emergency contact list

**Seasonal Additions**

- **Winter:** Extra thermal layers, insulated down jacket, warm gloves, microspikes
- **Monsoon:** Rain cover, waterproof dry bags, leech socks
- **Spring/Autumn:** Standard layering works well; bring a light down jacket for altitude

**Packing Tips**

- Keep essentials in your daypack—passport, cash, permits, water, snacks, and a warm layer.
- Use packing cubes or dry bags to stay organized.
- Wear your heaviest boots and jacket on travel days.
- Do not pack more than you can comfortably carry; porters have weight limits.
- Check your **Everest Base Camp packing list** or **Annapurna Circuit packing list** against the season you are trekking.

## FAQs

**Q: Is trekking in Nepal safe?**

**A:** Yes, with proper planning, a licensed guide, good travel insurance, and gradual acclimatization. The main risk is altitude sickness, but it is largely preventable by ascending slowly and taking rest days.

***

**Q: Do you need a guide to trek in Nepal?**

**A:** Yes, in most regions. Since 2023, solo independent trekking is restricted in many areas, and foreign trekkers must usually go through a registered agency with a licensed guide.

***

**Q: What is the best time to trek in Nepal?**

**A:** October–November and March–May are the best seasons for clear skies, stable weather, and excellent mountain views. Winter is quieter but colder, while monsoon is only suitable for select regions like Upper Mustang.

***

**Q: How much does it cost to trek in Nepal?**

**A:** Budget treks start around USD 40–70 per day. A 14-day guided trek from your home country often costs between USD 2,000 and USD 2,500 including the package, meals, visa, insurance, and flights.

***

**Q: What is the easiest trek in Nepal?**

**A:** Ghorepani Poon Hill and Mardi Himal are great for beginners. They are shorter, lower in altitude, and still offer spectacular Himalayan views.

***

**Q: What is the hardest trek in Nepal?**

**A:** Everest Three Passes, Manaslu Circuit, and Upper Mustang are among the hardest. They involve high passes, remote trails, and longer days at altitude.

***

**Q: Can I trek in Nepal without a guide?**

**A:** Only in limited areas, and even then, rules often require a registered agency. Check current regulations before you travel, or let Waveup Himalayas arrange everything for you.

***

**Q: How fit do I need to be to trek in Nepal?**

**A:** You should be able to walk 5–7 hours a day with a light pack. Hill walking, cardio, and strength training in the months before your trek will make the experience much more enjoyable.

***

**Q: What permits do I need for trekking in Nepal?**

**A:** It depends on the region. Everest Base Camp requires Sagarmatha National Park and Khumbu Pasang Lhamu permits. Annapurna routes require ACAP. Restricted areas like Upper Mustang and Manaslu require special permits.

***

**Q: Do I need travel insurance for trekking in Nepal?**

**A:** Yes. Your insurance must cover high-altitude trekking and helicopter evacuation. Mountain rescue in Nepal is expensive, and helicopters will not take off without guaranteed payment.

***

**Q: What should I pack for a Nepal trek?**

**A:** Layered clothing, waterproof hiking boots, a 4-season sleeping bag, trekking poles, a headlamp, water purification, a first-aid kit, and your permits and insurance details.
