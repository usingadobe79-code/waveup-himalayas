**Altitude sickness in Nepal** affects trekkers of every age and fitness level, and it is the main reason people turn back on routes such as Everest Base Camp or the Annapurna Circuit. The good news is that it is largely preventable. Most cases are mild, and the serious ones almost always follow the same pattern: climbing too fast, or ignoring the early warning signs.

We are WaveUp Himalayas, a trekking and tour company based in Thamel, Kathmandu. This guide explains the symptoms of altitude sickness, how to lower your risk before and during a trek, and what to do if you or someone in your group starts to feel unwell.

***

## What Is Altitude Sickness and Why Does It Happen?

Altitude sickness is your body's reaction to getting less oxygen as you gain height quickly. The air at 5,000 m contains the same percentage of oxygen as the air at sea level, but the lower air pressure means each breath delivers less of it. Your body can adapt by breathing faster and adjusting its blood and fluid balance, but that takes days, not hours.

Doctors describe three related conditions:

- **Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS):** the mild and common form.
- **High Altitude Cerebral Edema (HACE):** swelling of the brain. Rare, but life-threatening.
- **High Altitude Pulmonary Edema (HAPE):** fluid in the lungs. Also rare and life-threatening.

AMS is the one most trekkers will meet in some form. HACE and HAPE can develop from untreated AMS or come on quickly, and both need immediate descent.

***

## At What Altitude Does Altitude Sickness Start in Nepal?

Risk begins to rise from about 2,500 m and increases noticeably above 3,000 m. Many of the best-known trekking routes in the country go far beyond that, so altitude sickness in Nepal is something every trekker should plan for, not just climbers.

Two things matter more than the number itself: how fast you gain height, and how high you sleep.

## Altitude Sickness Symptoms

Knowing the symptoms of altitude sickness is the most useful safety skill you can bring on a trek. Learn them before you go, and check in with yourself every morning and evening.

**Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS) Symptoms**

AMS usually shows up 6 to 12 hours after arriving at a new altitude. The key symptom is a headache, combined with one or more of the following:

- Nausea or loss of appetite
- Unusual tiredness or weakness
- Dizziness or light-headedness
- Poor sleep

Mild AMS feels a lot like a heavy hangover. It is common and manageable if you stop climbing and rest.

**HACE Symptoms (Brain Swelling)**

HACE is an emergency. Watch for:

- Loss of balance or coordination (stumbling, can't walk a straight line)
- Confusion, odd behaviour or hallucinations
- Extreme drowsiness
- A headache that keeps getting worse

A simple check is to walk heel to toe along a straight line. If someone cannot do it, treat it as a warning sign and start descending.

**HAPE Symptoms (Fluid in the Lungs)**

HAPE is also an emergency. Watch for:

- Breathlessness at rest, not just on the climb
- A cough that gets worse, sometimes with pink or frothy sputum
- Chest tightness or a gurgling sound when breathing
- Unusual weakness and blue-tinged lips

HACE and HAPE can occur together, and they can progress quickly.

**Is It Altitude Sickness or Just Tiredness?**

Cold, dehydration, a stomach bug and plain exhaustion can all look like AMS. On a high-altitude trek, the safe rule is to assume your symptoms are altitude-related until proven otherwise. Don't go higher while you have symptoms, and be honest with your guide about how you feel. Many people hide symptoms so they don't slow the group, and that is how mild cases become serious ones.

## How to Prevent Altitude Sickness in Nepal

The most effective prevention is not a pill or a fitness plan. It is a slow, well-planned ascent.

**Go Up Slowly**

Above 3,000 m, try to raise your sleeping altitude by no more than about 300 to 500 m per day. Take a rest day every 3 to 4 days, or for roughly every 1,000 m you gain. Typical Everest Base Camp itineraries build in rest days at places such as Namche Bazaar and Dingboche for this reason. If an itinerary seems rushed, that is a red flag.

**Climb High, Sleep Low**

Spend the day hiking higher than where you will sleep, then come back down to rest. A short day hike above your lodge exposes your body to thinner air, while a lower bed helps it recover. This is the idea behind acclimatization hikes at stops like Namche Bazaar and Manang.

**Hydration, Food and Rest**

- Drink plenty of fluids. Around 3 to 4 litres a day is a common guideline.
- Eat regularly, even when your appetite dips. Carbohydrate-rich meals such as dal bhat are popular for good reason.
- Avoid alcohol and sleeping pills during your first nights at altitude.
- Walk slowly. The trek is not a race, and a steady pace is part of acclimatization.

**Diamox for Altitude Sickness: Ask Your Doctor First**

Acetazolamide, usually sold as Diamox, is a prescription medicine that can speed up acclimatization. A commonly used preventive dose is 125 mg twice a day, started the day before ascending above 3,000 m. It is not a substitute for a slow ascent, it can cause tingling fingers and more frequent urination, and it is not suitable for people with a sulfonamide allergy. Speak to your doctor or a travel clinic before you leave home about whether it suits you and what dose is right.

## Altitude Sickness Treatment on the Trail

What you do next depends on how severe the symptoms are. The golden rule is simple: never go higher with symptoms, and descend if they get worse.

**Mild AMS**

Stop climbing and rest at the same altitude. Drink fluids and take paracetamol or ibuprofen for the headache. If you feel better within 24 to 48 hours, you can continue slowly. If symptoms stay the same or worsen, go down.

**Moderate to Severe AMS**

Descend. Dropping 300 to 1,000 m is usually enough for symptoms to ease. Do not go back up until you feel fully recovered, and consider whether your itinerary needs to change.

**HACE and HAPE: Treat as an Emergency**

If you see signs of HACE or HAPE:

- **Descend immediately**, even at night, and never leave the person alone.
- Use supplemental oxygen if it is available. Some lodges and health posts along the main routes have oxygen or a portable hyperbaric chamber (a Gamow bag).
- Medicines such as dexamethasone (for HACE) and nifedipine (for HAPE) are used by medical professionals or trained trekkers. They support, but do not replace, descent.
- Arrange a helicopter evacuation if the person cannot walk down.

**Helicopter Evacuation and Travel Insurance**

Helicopters operate widely in Nepal, but a rescue can be expensive. Before you travel, check that your travel insurance explicitly covers trekking up to the maximum altitude of your route, and that it includes emergency helicopter evacuation. Keep a copy of your policy number with you on the trail.

## Altitude Sickness on Popular Nepal Treks

**Everest Base Camp and Kala Patthar**

Everest Base Camp sits at 5,364 m, and Kala Patthar, at 5,545 m, is higher still. The trail climbs steadily from Lukla, so patience in the first few days pays off later. Longer variations such as the [Everest Base Camp Trek with Gokyo Lake](https://waveuphimalayas.com/packages/everest-base-camp-gokyo-lake-trek/) or the [Three Passes Trek](https://waveuphimalayas.com/packages/everest-base-camp-three-passes-trek/) spend more time high, so they suit people who are comfortable at altitude. For the highest viewpoint, read our [Kala Patthar trek guide](https://waveuphimalayas.com/blog/kala-patthar-trek-guide-height-cost-best-time/).

A helicopter ride to the Khumbu skips acclimatization entirely, so anyone considering one should still know the symptoms. The [Everest Base Camp Heli Tour](https://waveuphimalayas.com/packages/everest-base-camp-heli-tour/) is an option for those who want the views without the long walk.

**Annapurna Circuit and Thorong La Pass**

The [Annapurna Circuit Trek](https://waveuphimalayas.com/packages/annapurna-circuit-trek/) crosses Thorong La at 5,416 m. The crossing is a long day, and the pass is reached after several days of steady height gain, so acclimatization around Manang is important. Start early, move at a steady pace, and avoid crossing if you feel unwell.

**Manaslu Circuit**

The [Manaslu Circuit Trek](https://waveuphimalayas.com/packages/manaslu-circuit-trek/) crosses Larkya La at 5,106 m through remote Tibetan-influenced villages. Because the region is remote, getting help takes longer, which makes a conservative pace and early reporting of symptoms even more important.

**Lower-Altitude Options**

If altitude is your main worry, shorter routes are a sensible start. Poon Hill (3,210 m) and Annapurna Base Camp (4,130 m) both give big mountain scenery without going above 5,000 m. Altitude sickness in Nepal is still possible on these routes, particularly at Annapurna Base Camp, but it is less likely when you ascend gradually.

## Who Is at Higher Risk of Altitude Sickness?

Anyone can be affected, but the risk is higher if you:

- Have had altitude illness on a previous trip
- Fly or drive quickly to a high altitude
- Ascend too fast for your body
- Have a heart, lung or blood condition
- Are pregnant

If any of these apply, or if you are unsure, see a doctor who knows about altitude before booking. Neither age nor fitness predicts who will be affected, which is why even experienced hikers should follow the same rules.
