If you are planning the trek of a lifetime, understanding **Everest Base Camp accommodation and food** is one of the most practical steps you can take before you fly to Nepal. The good news is that you will not be camping or surviving on energy bars. The standard EBC route is a teahouse trek, meaning you sleep in family-run mountain lodges and eat freshly cooked meals every day, all the way from Lukla to Gorak Shep. The experience is simple, warm, and deeply connected to Sherpa hospitality. But it does change as you climb, and knowing what to expect prevents the common disappointment of imagining hotel comfort at 5,000 metres.

This guide explains exactly what teahouse rooms are like, what food is available, how much you should budget, and the small etiquette points that make your trek smoother. It is written for trekkers preparing for the Everest Base Camp trek with Wave Up Himalayas, so you can arrive informed and ready to enjoy the journey.

***

## What Is a Teahouse on the Everest Base Camp Trek?

A teahouse is a family-run lodge that provides both a bed and meals. At the centre of every teahouse is the dining room, usually with a stove burning yak dung, wood, or kerosene, surrounded by benches and tables where trekkers eat, charge devices, and warm up in the evening. Bedrooms are typically in a separate wing or on an upper floor. Most teahouses are owned and run by local Sherpa families who live on site and cook the meals themselves.

![](/images/blog/teahouses-everest-trek-1724649609.jpg "Tea Houses in EBC")

Because the business model depends on food sales, room prices are kept intentionally low, and guests are expected to eat breakfast and dinner where they sleep. This “eat where you sleep” custom is important teahouse etiquette, and it shapes how prices work across the trail. You do not need a tent at any point on the standard route, because there is a teahouse at every overnight stop from Lukla to Gorak Shep.

## Everest Base Camp Accommodation: How Rooms Change with Altitude

The single most useful thing to understand about [**Everest Base Camp Trek** ](https://waveuphimalayas.com/packages/everest-base-camp-trek/)accommodation is that comfort decreases steadily as you gain altitude. The drop is not gradual; it happens in fairly distinct steps tied to specific villages. Knowing this progression helps you pack correctly and adjust your expectations.

### Lower Villages: Lukla, Phakding, and Namche Bazaar

![](/images/blog/Everest-Summit-Lodges-Lukla-room-001.jpg "Room in Lukla")

In Lukla (2,840m) and Namche Bazaar (3,440m), teahouses are at their most comfortable. Many rooms are twin-share with the option of private bathrooms, and some lodges have solar or hydro-powered electricity. Hot showers are usually included or available at low cost. In Namche, you will also find upmarket “luxury lodges” with attached bathrooms, electric blankets, and better insulation, though these cost significantly more than standard teahouses.

### Middle Trail: Tengboche to Dingboche

From Tengboche (3,860m) through Dingboche (4,360m), twin rooms with shared bathrooms become standard. Hot showers usually cost an additional NPR 200 to 400, and charging and Wi-Fi become paid extras. Rooms are simple: two single beds, a thin foam mattress, a pillow, and sometimes a blanket. Walls are often plywood, so you will hear your neighbours. Rooms are not heated anywhere on the trail; warmth is concentrated in the communal dining room.

### High Altitude: Lobuche and Gorak Shep

Above Lobuche (4,930m) and especially at Gorak Shep (5,160m), teahouses become genuinely basic. Expect thin-walled twin rooms with a foam mattress, shared outdoor facilities, and no hot showers at all. This is expedition-grade simplicity. The blanket alone is rarely enough above Dingboche, which is exactly why a sleeping bag rated to at least minus 10 degrees Celsius is essential gear from Day 6 onward. Understanding this progression prevents the common mistake of expecting Namche-level comfort at Gorak Shep, when the two villages are entirely different tiers of accommodation.

## Everest Base Camp Food: What You Will Eat on the Trail

First-time trekkers often assume the food on the Everest Base Camp trek will be a genuine hardship—something to endure between the more rewarding parts of the day. That assumption is wrong. Teahouse kitchens along the trail produce genuinely good, filling meals every day, cooked fresh to order, with real variety even at altitude. The food does change as you climb, becoming simpler and more repetitive above 4,000 metres, but calling it a hardship undersells what cooks manage to produce in difficult conditions with limited supplies.

Food on the EBC trail is not just about feeling full. It is required to maintain your energy levels, assist in acclimatisation, and stay healthy at high altitude. Knowing what to eat, when to eat it, and how the menu shifts as you gain elevation is one of the most practical preparations you can make.

### Dal Bhat: The Single Best Food for This Altitude

![](/images/blog/Copy-of-Untitled-Design-31-1024x576.jpg "dal bhat")

**Dal bhat** is the Nepali staple of lentils, rice, and vegetable curry, and it is genuinely the best food available for trekking at altitude. There is real nutritional logic behind that claim. The lentils provide protein and iron, both important for the increased red blood cell production your body undergoes as it acclimatises to reduced oxygen. The rice provides easily digestible carbohydrates for sustained energy on long trekking days. The accompanying broth and vegetable curry contribute meaningfully to daily hydration, which matters enormously at altitude where dehydration compounds the effects of reduced oxygen.

Nearly every teahouse offers **unlimited dal bhat refills at no extra charge**, a tradition that exists specifically because trekkers and porters alike need the calories. It is one of the best-value, most functional meals you will eat anywhere on the trip. Prices typically range from **NPR 700 to 1,400** depending on altitude, and it remains the best value meal on the mountain. For trekkers eating dal bhat for two meals a day, the savings compared to Western menu selections can be $8 to $15 daily.

### Breakfast on the Trail

![](/images/blog/luxury-everest-base-camp-trek-food-celebration-meal.jpg "Breakfast in Everest Base Camp Trek")

Breakfast at teahouses is fairly consistent across the whole route: **porridge, pancakes, toast with jam or honey, and eggs prepared however you prefer**—fried, boiled, or as an omelette—form the standard menu at nearly every stop. Tibetan bread, a slightly denser fried bread, is also common and a good source of sustained energy before a long trekking day.

Coffee and tea, both black and milk tea, are available everywhere, though **instant coffee is far more common than fresh brewed** at altitude given the logistics of supplying anything more elaborate this far up the valley. Most trekkers pre-order breakfast the night before so it is ready shortly after waking, which helps with an early start.

A hearty breakfast matters because it needs to fuel five to eight hours of trekking before your next proper meal. If your appetite is low, porridge, eggs, and a hot drink are a steady combination. Do not start a walking day with only coffee—above 4,000 metres, poor fuelling catches up quickly.

### Lunch and Dinner: What to Expect

Lunch is usually taken at a teahouse along the trail, while dinner is eaten at your overnight lodge. Lower down, menus are more varied. Common lunch and dinner options include **dal bhat, fried rice, fried noodles, vegetable soup, potatoes, pasta, momos, garlic soup, and egg dishes**.

Momos are steamed or fried dumplings and are widely available, with vegetable options being common and naturally vegetarian or vegan. Pasta and pizza appear at many teahouses, especially in the lower villages, though both become more basic at higher altitude. Fried rice and noodles are common, filling options that are easy to digest.

Lunch should not be too heavy, oily, or cold, because you still need to walk in the afternoon. Many trekkers choose dal bhat, fried rice, noodle soup, or potatoes because they are warm, steady, and easy to digest.

### Garlic Soup and Other High-Altitude Favourites

**Garlic soup** becomes a fixture on nearly every trekker’s order from around Day 5 onward. There is a traditional belief among Sherpa communities that garlic aids circulation and helps with acclimatisation, and regardless of the mechanism, a hot bowl of garlic soup is comforting and hydrating at altitude. It is also one of the cheapest items on the menu, typically priced between NPR 300 and 700.

**Thukpa**, a Tibetan noodle soup with vegetables or egg, is another high-altitude favourite—filling and easy to digest. **Sherpa stew** is a hearty noodle-and-vegetable soup that appears on many menus and is particularly welcome on cold evenings.

### How the Menu Changes with Altitude

The single most useful thing to understand about Everest Base Camp food is that **variety and freshness decrease steadily as you gain altitude**, while prices increase. This is not a marketing choice; it is a supply-chain reality. Every item above Namche Bazaar travels either by yak or by porter, and fuel costs at 5,000 metres run several times higher than in Kathmandu. 

**Lower Khumbu — Lukla, Phakding, Namche Bazaar (2,840m–3,440m)**
Most variety on the whole route. Fresh greens, tomatoes, cauliflower and carrots are common. Chicken, eggs, buffalo and lentils are all available. Expect momos, pizza, pasta, dal bhat and fried rice on the menu.

**Mid Trail — Tengboche, Pangboche, Dingboche (3,860m–4,360m)**
The menu tightens. Fresh produce is mostly potatoes, onions, garlic and cabbage. Eggs, lentils and the occasional tinned meat. Dal bhat, thukpa, garlic soup and fried noodles dominate.

**High Trail — Lobuche, Gorak Shep (4,930m–5,160m)**
The simplest food on the trek. Potatoes and stored root vegetables are about all the "fresh" produce left. Eggs, lentils and tinned goods. Thukpa, dal bhat, porridge and garlic soup are your realistic options.

AT the highest villages, menus narrow considerably. Dal bhat, thukpa, garlic soup, and porridge become the mainstays. This is exactly why experienced guides often recommend **switching to vegetarian meals above Namche**. Fresh meat cannot survive the journey without refrigeration, and plant-based dishes are more likely to be cooked fresh to order.

### **Vegetarian and Vegan Options**

The EBC trek is one of the easiest high-altitude routes in the world for vegetarians and vegans. Every teahouse along the trail serves **dal bhat, vegetable noodles, fried rice, momos, soup, and potato dishes** every single day.

Dal bhat is completely vegetarian by default. For vegan trekkers, simply ask for **no ghee and no yogurt**. Vegetable fried rice (without egg or butter), vegetable fried noodles, vegetable momos, and garlic soup are all vegan-friendly. The menu is built around plants already, so vegetarian food is not an afterthought but the backbone of teahouse cooking.

### Drinks and Hydration

At altitude, aim to drink **three to four litres of fluid per day** to help prevent altitude sickness. This includes water, tea, and soup. **Boiled water** is available for purchase at teahouses, and prices rise with altitude. Purification tablets are a lighter, cheaper backup. Avoid untreated tap water or stream water.

Hot lemon tea, ginger-honey-lemon tea, and hot chocolate are popular choices alongside black and milk tea. Bottled water becomes expensive at higher elevations and creates plastic waste, so a reusable bottle with purification tablets is the more practical and responsible choice.

## Everest Base Camp Food Costs and Daily Budget

A realistic food budget for the Everest Base Camp trek is around **USD 25 to 45 per person per day** for breakfast, lunch, and dinner, plus basic drinks. Your actual spending depends on what you order, how many hot drinks you have, and how much you eat at higher elevations. For a 10 to 12 day trek, a full food budget of roughly USD 250 to 540 is a reasonable planning range.

Prices rise steadily with altitude because every supply, from rice to gas, must be carried up by porters or yaks. A plate of dal bhat at low altitude might cost NPR 700, while at Gorak Shep the same dish can reach NPR 1,400. Hot showers, device charging, and Wi-Fi are also charged separately and increase with altitude. These are not hidden costs; they are simply part of how remote lodges operate.

## Practical Tips for Teahouse Life and Trekking Food

A few simple habits make teahouse life smoother and more enjoyable.

**Eat where you sleep.** Room rates are low because lodges rely on food sales. Having at least dinner and breakfast at your lodge is standard and expected.

**Pre-order breakfast.** Most teahouses ask you to order breakfast the night before so it is ready shortly after you wake. This speeds up the morning routine, especially on trekking days when you want an early start.

**Stay hydrated deliberately.** At altitude, aim to drink three to four litres of fluid per day. Boiled water is available for purchase, and prices rise with altitude. Purification tablets are a lighter, cheaper backup.

**Carry enough cash.** There are no ATMs above Namche Bazaar. Ensure you have enough Nepali Rupees to cover food, hot showers, charging, and any treats for the remainder of the trek after Namche.

**Pack a good sleeping bag.** Rooms are unheated everywhere on the trail. A bag rated to at least minus 10 degrees Celsius is essential above Dingboche, and a liner adds warmth and cleanliness.
