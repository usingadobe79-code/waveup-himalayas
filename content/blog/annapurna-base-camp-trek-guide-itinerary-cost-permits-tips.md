## Best Time to Trek  Annapurna Base Camp

Choosing the right season is one of the most important decisions for your **Annapurna Base Camp Trek**. The weather, visibility, trail conditions, and even teahouse availability vary dramatically throughout the year. Here’s a complete breakdown to help you pick the perfect window for your **ABC Trek Nepal**.

**Autumn (September–November)**

![](/images/blog/trekkers-at-annapurna-base-camp-autumn-season.jpg-001.webp)

Autumn is widely considered the **best time to trek to Annapurna Base Camp**. The monsoon has cleared, leaving crisp, clean air and spectacular mountain views. **October** offers the best visibility of the entire year, with **Annapurna I**, **Machhapuchhre**, and **Annapurna South** appearing sharp and close.

- **Pros:** Stable weather, clear skies, comfortable temperatures, vibrant teahouse atmosphere.
- **Cons:** Busy trails, higher demand for guides and lodges.
- **Tip:** Book your **Annapurna Base Camp Trek Package** well in advance for October and November.

**Spring (March–May)**

![](/images/blog/images%20%288%29.jpg)

Spring is the second-best season for the **Annapurna Base Camp Trek**. The trail between **Ghandruk** and **Chhomrong** erupts in rhododendron blooms, creating one of the most beautiful forest walks in Nepal. Temperatures are milder than autumn, though haze can reduce visibility in April and May.

- **Pros:** Beautiful flowers, warmer weather, good trail conditions.
- **Cons:** Slightly hazier views, occasional pre-monsoon showers.
- **Best months:** March and early April for the best combination of flowers and views.

**Winter (December–February)** 

![](/images/blog/abc-trekking%20%281%29.webp)

Winter offers solitude on the trail, but conditions are harsh. At **Annapurna Base Camp (4,130m)**, temperatures can drop below -20°C, and some teahouses close. Only experienced trekkers with proper winter gear should attempt the trek during this season.

- **Pros:** Fewer crowds, peaceful experience.
- **Cons:** Extreme cold, snow-blocked trails, limited services.

**Monsoon (June–August)** 

![](/images/blog/annapurna-base-camp-trek-during-monsoon.webp)

The monsoon brings heavy rain, leeches, landslides, and cloudy skies that hide the mountains. Most trekking agencies, including ours, do not operate the **ABC Trek** during peak monsoon. If you must trek, late September is your best bet as the rains taper off.

No matter when you go, the **Annapurna Base Camp Trek** is a life-changing experience. Plan around the seasons, prepare for the altitude, and you’ll be rewarded with memories that last a lifetime.

## Annapurna Base Camp Trek Elevation

The **Annapurna Base Camp (ABC) Trek** takes you from Nepal’s green foothills to an impressive altitude of **4,130 metres (13,550 feet)**. Understanding the elevation helps you plan your trek, prepare for altitude changes, and enjoy the journey safely.

![](/images/blog/6d3e1881-f638-45e3-8271-f72ef9bf3dbe%20%281%29.png "ABC Trek Elevation")

**Key Altitude Stops**

- Nayapul: 1,070 m
- Ghorepani: 2,860 m
- Poon Hill (optional): 3,210 m
- Chhomrong: 2,170 m
- Deurali: 3,230 m
- Machhapuchhre Base Camp: 3,700 m
- Annapurna Base Camp: 4,130 m

**Altitude Sickness and Safety**

Altitude sickness can occur above 2,500 metres, with increasing risk at higher elevations. Watch for headaches, nausea, dizziness, and unusual fatigue. Ascend gradually, walk at a comfortable pace, eat regularly, and inform your guide if you feel unwell. If symptoms worsen, do not ascend further; descend and seek medical help when needed.

**Final Thoughts**

Reaching **Annapurna Base Camp at 4,130 metres** offers breathtaking views of Annapurna I and the surrounding Himalayas. With proper planning, gradual acclimatization, and awareness of altitude sickness, you can enjoy this unforgettable adventure with greater confidence.

## **Annapurna Base Camp Trek Permits**

Every trekker entering the Annapurna Sanctuary needs the right permits—and since April 2023, a licensed guide as well. At **WaveUp Himalayas**, we arrange all of this for you, but here is what you need to know.

**ACAP — The Essential Permit**

The **Annapurna Conservation Area Permit (ACAP)** is mandatory. It costs **NPR 3,000** for foreign nationals and covers the entire Annapurna region. It is checked at Birethanti and again at Chhomrong. Your fee directly funds trail maintenance, waste management, and community projects across the area.

**TIMS — Confirm Before You Go**

The **TIMS card** (NPR 1,000 with a guide, NPR 2,000 solo) has been inconsistently enforced in recent years. Some sources say it is no longer required for Annapurna; others say it still is. Our advice: confirm at the time of booking. If in doubt, get both.

**Mandatory Guide Rule**

Since **April 1, 2023**, all foreign trekkers on ABC routes must trek with a **licensed guide** booked through a registered Nepali agency. Your guide's TAAN license number must appear on your permit application at issue—you cannot add it later. This is why your guide must be arranged **before** you visit the permit office.

**What to Bring**

- Passport (valid 6+ months) and current Nepal visa
- **Four passport-size photos** (two per permit)
- Your guide's TAAN license number
- Nepali rupees in cash

**Where to Get Them**

- **Pokhara:** NTNC office, Lakeside (most convenient for ABC)
- **Kathmandu:** NTNC office, Bhrikutimandap
- **Online:** ACAP only via [epermit.ntnc.org.np](https://epermit.ntnc.org.np/) (2.9% card fee)
- **Do not wait until the trail:** checkpoint officers charge double.

When you trek with **WaveUp Himalayas**, your ACAP permit, TIMS card, and guide's TAAN number are arranged before you arrive. You focus on the mountains. We handle the paperwork.

## Annapurna Base Camp Trek Itinerary

The Annapurna Base Camp Trek typically takes **7 to 12 days**, depending on your starting point, pace, and itinerary. Below is a classic **10-day itinerary** — the most popular route via Chhomrong and Jhinu Danda.

**Day 1: Kathmandu → Pokhara**

- **Altitude:** 822 m
- **Travel:** 6–7 hours drive / 25 minutes flight
- **Highlights:** Scenic journey through green hills, winding rivers, terraced fields, and traditional villages. Relax by Phewa Lake in Pokhara and enjoy Annapurna views from Lakeside.

**Day 2: Pokhara → Jhinu → Chhomrong**

- **Altitude:** 2,170 m
- **Trek:** 2–3 hours drive + 5–6 hours trek
- **Highlights:** Drive to Jhinu, then trek to Chhomrong — a traditional Gurung village with spectacular mountain views.

**Day 3: Chhomrong → Bamboo**

- **Altitude:** 2,310 m
- **Trek:** 5–6 hours
- **Highlights:** Descend the famous stone steps and trek through lush forests along the Modi Khola river.

**Day 4: Bamboo → Deurali**

- **Altitude:** 3,200 m
- **Trek:** 5–6 hours
- **Highlights:** Walk through bamboo and rhododendron forest as the trail gradually transitions to alpine terrain.

**Day 5: Deurali → Machhapuchhre Base Camp → Annapurna Base Camp**

- **Altitude:** 4,130 m
- **Trek:** 5–6 hours
- **Highlights:** Trek to Machhapuchhre Base Camp, then continue to Annapurna Base Camp. Enjoy the stunning 360° panorama of Annapurna I, Annapurna South, Hiunchuli, and Machhapuchhre.

**Day 6: Annapurna Base Camp → Bamboo**

- **Altitude:** 2,310 m
- **Trek:** 6–7 hours
- **Highlights:** Catch the sunrise at ABC, then descend through valleys and forests back to Bamboo.

**Day 7: Bamboo → Jhinu Danda**

- **Altitude:** 1,780 m
- **Trek:** 5–6 hours
- **Highlights:** Trek down to Jhinu Danda and relax in the natural riverside hot springs — the perfect reward after reaching Base Camp.

**Day 8: Jhinu → Pokhara**

- **Altitude:** 822 m
- **Trek:** 3 hours trek + 2–3 hours drive
- **Highlights:** Short trek and drive back to Pokhara. Celebrate your achievement by the lake.

**Day 9: Pokhara → Kathmandu**

- **Altitude:** 1,400 m
- **Travel:** 6–7 hours drive / 25 minutes flight
- **Highlights:** Return to Kathmandu. Optional sightseeing or rest.

**Day 10: Final Departure**

- **Transfer:** Airport transfer for onward journey.

**Shorter & Longer Options**

- **7-day ABC Trek:** Skip the Kathmandu–Pokhara travel days. Start directly from Pokhara and use a faster route via Ghandruk or Kimche.
- **12-day ABC Trek:** Add extra acclimatization nights at Deurali or MBC, or include the Poon Hill extension via Ghorepani.
- **ABC with Poon Hill:** Add 2–3 days for the Ghorepani and Poon Hill sunrise (3,210 m).

## Annapurna Base Camp Trek Difficulty

The **Annapurna Base Camp Trek Difficulty** is best described as **moderate**. It is not technical—no ropes, no crampons, no climbing experience required—but it is a genuine Himalayan trek that demands respect and preparation.

**Difficulty at a Glance**

- **Grade:** Moderate
- **Max altitude:** 4,130 m
- **Daily walking:** 5–7 hours
- **Total distance:** Roughly 100–115 km round trip
- **Terrain:** Stone staircases, steep ascents and descents, river valleys, alpine paths

**What Makes It Challenging**

**The stairs.** The ABC trail is famous for its long stone staircases, especially around Chhomrong and the descent to Jhinu Danda. They are hard on the knees and calves, and trekking poles make a real difference.

**The altitude.** Above 3,000 m, the air thins noticeably. Deurali, MBC, and ABC are the stages where most trekkers feel it. A gradual itinerary and steady hydration are your best protection.

**The daily hours.** Five to seven hours of walking per day, day after day, is the real test. Stamina matters more than speed.

**The cold.** Teahouses are unheated and nights at altitude drop well below freezing. A proper sleeping bag and down jacket are essential.

**What Makes It Achievable**

The trail is **well marked and well served** by teahouses, so you are never far from shelter, food, and a bed. The ascent profile is gradual enough for most fit trekkers to acclimatize naturally over 7–12 days. And unlike Everest Base Camp, there are no domestic flights and no extreme altitude to contend with.

**Who Can Do It**

If you can comfortably walk **15–20 km in a day** and have trained for **4–6 weeks** with cardio and stairs, you can complete the ABC Trek. Age matters far less than fitness, and we have guided trekkers in their sixties to Base Camp.

**How to Make It Easier**

- Train with stairs and a weighted pack before you come
- Add acclimatization nights—a 10–12 day itinerary is kinder than a rushed 7-day push
- Hire a porter to carry your heavy pack
- Walk slowly and steadily; "bistari bistari" is the local wisdom
- Listen to your body and descend if altitude symptoms worsen

**Altitude Sickness**

AMS is a real risk above 3,000 m. Watch for headaches, nausea, dizziness, and breathlessness at rest. Drink 3–4 litres of water daily, avoid alcohol at altitude, and never push through worsening symptoms. Our guides carry oximeters and first-aid kits and are trained to act quickly.

## Food, Accommodation & Water on the Annapurna Base Camp Trek

Life on the ABC trail runs on Nepal's famous **teahouse system**—family-run mountain lodges that have served trekkers for generations. Understanding how they work, what you will eat, and how to stay hydrated will make your trek far more comfortable. Here is what to expect when you trek with **WaveUp Himalayas**.

### Accommodation: The Teahouse Experience

A teahouse is part guesthouse, part family home, and part community hub. Most are built from stone and painted in bright blues, greens, and whites, with prayer flags strung across the front and a warm dining hall at the centre of daily life.

![](/images/blog/annapurna-base-camp-trail-001.webp "Tea House in ABC Trek")

**Your room** is simple and functional: two single beds, a pillow, and thick blankets folded at the foot. Walls are thin—sometimes just plywood—so you will hear your neighbours. There is no heating in the rooms, and no insulation to speak of. At higher altitudes, the temperature inside can drop close to freezing at night. This is why we insist every trekker carries a sleeping bag rated for **-10°C to -15°C**, along with a down jacket for the evenings.

**The dining hall** is the heart of every teahouse. It is the only heated space, warmed by a central stove—often fuelled by wood or dried yak dung—where trekkers, guides, and porters gather to eat, dry wet clothes, charge devices, and swap stories. Meals are eaten here, not in your room. In the higher villages, the stove is lit only in the evening, so the hall fills quickly and becomes the social centre of the trek.

**Bathrooms** are shared and basic. Lower down, you may find a squat toilet with running water; higher up, expect a simple drop toilet and a bucket. Hot showers are available at most teahouses for an extra charge, usually in a separate cubicle with a gas heater. They become less reliable the higher you climb—at MBC and ABC, a hot shower may not be available at all.

**Electricity and connectivity** exist but are limited. Solar power is common, and charging your devices costs extra—more at altitude, where power is scarcer. Wi-Fi is available in most lower villages but slow and often unusable above Deurali. Treat connectivity as a bonus, not a guarantee, and download your maps, music, and reading material before you leave Pokhara.

**A cultural note:** teahouses are family businesses, and the people who run them are your hosts, not hotel staff. A smile, a few words of Nepali, and patience during busy hours go a long way. In peak season, lodges at Chhomrong, Bamboo, and Deurali fill by mid-afternoon—which is why we start walking early and secure beds before the crowds arrive.

### Food: Fuel for the Mountains

The food on the ABC trek is simple, hearty, and surprisingly varied given how remote these kitchens are. Everything is carried up by hand, mule, or porter, so menus shrink as you gain altitude—but you will never go hungry.

![](/images/blog/Food-on-the-Annapurna-Base-Camp-Trek.png "Annapurna Base Camp")

**Dal bhat** is the foundation of the trek. It is a platter of steamed rice, lentil soup, seasonal vegetables, and pickle, and it is what most Nepalis eat twice a day. It is nutritious, balanced, and often **refillable**—the words _"dal bhat power, 24 hour"_ are a genuine trekking truth. If you eat one meal on the trail, make it this one.

**Breakfast** usually means Tibetan bread or chapati with honey, jam, or peanut butter, eggs prepared to order, porridge, muesli, or pancakes. **Trekking breakfast sets**—egg, toast, potato, and tea—are common and filling.

**Lunch and dinner menus** typically include:

- **Noodle dishes** — fried noodles, noodle soup (thukpa), and pasta with tomato or cheese sauce
- **Momo** — steamed dumplings filled with vegetables or cheese, served with spicy chutney
- **Soups** — garlic soup is a trekker's favourite, reputed to help with altitude
- **Rice and curry dishes** — curries, fried rice, and vegetable sekuwa
- **Potato dishes** — fried potatoes, potato curry, and chips
- **Pizza and burgers** — available at lower teahouses, though quality varies
- **Desserts** — apple pie, chocolate pudding, and pancakes with honey or chocolate

**Tea** is the fuel of the trail. Milk tea, black tea, lemon tea, ginger tea, and masala tea are available everywhere. Ginger tea is especially welcome on cold mornings and is believed to help with digestion and altitude. Coffee, hot chocolate, and hot lemon with honey are also common.

**One important rule: avoid meat above Chhomrong.** There is no refrigeration at altitude, and meat is either carried up unrefrigerated or slaughtered on-site without proper cold storage. Vegetarian dishes are freshly prepared and far safer. Eggs are generally fine, as they keep well and are cooked to order.

**Vegetarian and vegan trekkers** are well served—dal bhat, noodles, potatoes, and vegetable curries form the backbone of the menu, and most kitchens will adapt dishes on request. If you have allergies or strict dietary needs, tell your guide in advance so we can brief the teahouses along your route.

### Water: Your Most Important Habit

Safe hydration is the single most important daily habit on the ABC trek—and the easiest one to get wrong.

**Never drink tap water or untreated stream water.** The water in the Annapurna region carries bacteria and parasites that will end your trek quickly. You have three safe options:

**Boiled water** from teahouses is widely available and reliable. It is served hot, sometimes in a flask, and is the simplest solution if you prefer not to carry purification equipment.

**Purification tablets or a filter bottle** are the cheapest, lightest, and most environmentally responsible choice. A filter bottle lets you refill from teahouse taps and streams safely, saving money and eliminating plastic waste. We strongly recommend this option and can advise on the best models before your trek.

**Sealed bottled water** is available in most villages but is expensive, contributes to plastic pollution in the mountains, and is often just filtered tap water anyway. We discourage it, though it remains a fallback in an emergency.

**How much to drink:** aim for **3–4 litres a day**, and more as you climb above 3,000 m. Drink steadily throughout the day rather than in large amounts at once. Check your urine colour—pale yellow means you are well hydrated; dark yellow means drink more.

**Avoid alcohol above 3,000 m.** It dehydrates you, disrupts sleep, and worsens the effects of altitude. Save the celebration for Pokhara.

### Practical Comforts

Hot showers, Wi-Fi, and device charging are available at most teahouses for an additional charge, which rises with altitude. They are welcome luxuries, not guarantees—expect them lower down, and be prepared to go without at MBC and ABC.

**Cash is essential.** There are no ATMs beyond Nayapul, and every teahouse, shop, and hot shower is paid in Nepali rupees. Carry enough cash from Pokhara to cover your entire trek, plus a buffer.

## Annapurna Base Camp Trek Packing List

Pack for four seasons in one trek. Keep it light—your daypack under 7 kg, your duffle under 15 kg if you hire a porter.

**Clothing**

- **Base layers:** 2–3 moisture-wicking tops, thermal leggings, underwear (never cotton)
- **Mid layers:** Fleece jacket, lightweight down jacket, warm down jacket for evenings
- **Outer layers:** Waterproof jacket with hood, waterproof trousers, pack rain cover
- **Trousers:** 2 quick-drying trekking pairs, 1 warm evening pair
- **Footwear:** Broken-in waterproof boots, camp sandals, 3–4 trekking socks, liner socks
- **Head & hands:** Beanie, sun hat, buff, insulated gloves, liner gloves
- **Accessories:** UV400 sunglasses

**Gear**

- Sleeping bag rated **-10°C to -15°C** + liner (teahouses are unheated)
- Trekking poles—essential for the stone staircases
- Headlamp with spare batteries
- Daypack (25–35 L) + duffle for porter
- Water filter bottle or purification tablets
- 1–2 insulated water bottles, electrolyte sachets
- High-energy snacks—nuts, chocolate, energy bars
- Power bank (10,000–20,000 mAh), charging cables, adapter
- Phone with offline maps downloaded, camera with spare batteries

**Health & Personal Care**

- Personal first-aid kit—plasters, blister care, antiseptic, painkillers
- Diamox (only if prescribed), regular medication, rehydration salts
- Biodegradable soap, toothbrush, small towel
- Toilet paper and hand sanitiser—teahouses often run out
- Sunscreen SPF 50+, lip balm with SPF
- Menstrual products if needed

**Documents & Money**

- Passport (6+ months valid), Nepal visa, passport photos
- Travel insurance covering trekking to 4,130 m
- Cash in Nepali rupees—no ATMs past Nayapul

**Optional**

- Earplugs and eye mask, trekking umbrella, duct tape, zip-lock bags, padlock, book

**Leave Behind**

- Cotton clothing, unboken boots, bulky toiletries, hairdryer, drone without permit

**WaveUp Himalayas Tips**

- **Break in your boots** at least 4 weeks before
- **Layer, don't bulk**—lighter layers beat one heavy jacket
- **Rent** down jackets and sleeping bags in Pokhara
- **Charge everything** before leaving Pokhara
