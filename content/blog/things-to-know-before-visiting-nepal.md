## Nepal Visa & Entry Requirements

Most travelers can get a **Nepal visa on arrival** at Tribhuvan International Airport in Kathmandu and at major land border crossings. Citizens of the **US, UK, EU, Australia, Canada**, and many other countries qualify. **Indian citizens do not need a visa** to enter Nepal.

**Nepal Visa Fees (2026)**

- **15 days:** USD 30
- **30 days:** USD 50
- **90 days:** USD 125

All tourist visas are **multiple-entry**.

**How to Apply**

- Use the **Nepal online visa** form within 15 days of arrival.
- Print the barcode receipt and bring it with you to skip long queues.
- Carry **new, crisp USD bills**—old or torn notes may be rejected.

**Nepal Entry Requirements**

- Passport valid for at least **6 months**
- At least **2 blank pages**
- Proof of onward travel
- Proof of accommodation
- Passport-sized photos
- Visa fee payment

**Nepal Customs Rules**

- Declare cash over **USD 5,000**
- Alcohol limit: **1.15 liters**
- Tobacco limit: **200 cigarettes**
- Gold: up to **50 grams**; Silver: up to **100 grams**
- **Drones require special permits** and may be confiscated without one

**Nepal Visa Extension**

- Apply at the Department of Immigration in **Kathmandu or Pokhara**
- Fee: **USD 3 per day**
- Maximum stay: **150 days per calendar year**

**Nepal visa on arrival** is easy: apply online, carry USD cash, and meet **entry and customs rules**. That's it.

## Best Time to Visit Nepal

### Autumn (Mid-October to Mid-November)

Autumn is widely considered the **[best time to visit Nepal,](https://waveuphimalayas.com/blog/best-time-to-visit-nepal-wave-up-himalayas/)** offering clear skies, stable weather, and vibrant festivals.

![Autumn (Mid-October to Mid-November)](/images/blog/Autumn%20trkking%20in%20Nepal%20%281%29.jpg "Autumn (Mid-October to Mid-November)")

- **Weather:** Dry, stable, and pleasant at lower altitudes; cold at night above 3,000m.
- **Mountain Views:** The sharpest and clearest of the year—ideal for photography and sightseeing.
- **Trekking Conditions:** Trails are dry, firm, and in excellent shape; high passes are open.
- **Best Treks & Places:** Everest Base Camp, Annapurna Circuit, Langtang, Kathmandu Valley, Pokhara, and Chitwan.
- **Crowds & Prices:** Peak season with busy trails and highest prices—book flights, teahouses, and permits early.

Autumn offers the best overall conditions for trekking and mountain views, making it the top choice for most travelers.

### Spring (March to May)

Spring is the **second-best time to visit Nepal**, featuring blooming rhododendrons, warming weather, and Everest summit season.

![](/images/blog/1717482117_TMVrf_phewa-lake.opt-001.webp)

- **Weather:** Warming across the country; pleasant days at lower altitudes, cold at higher elevations.
- **Mountain Views:** Generally clear in March and early April; haze and dust build below 3,000m by late April.
- **Trekking Conditions:** Trails are in good shape; snow may remain on high passes in March and early April.
- **Best Treks & Places:** Everest Base Camp, Annapurna Region, Poon Hill, Langtang, Chitwan, and Upper Mustang.
- **Crowds & Prices:** Second busiest season; high prices and steady traffic on popular routes.

Spring is ideal for rhododendron lovers, photographers, and Everest enthusiasts seeking vibrant landscapes and lively trails.

### Monsoon (June to September)

Monsoon is the **worst time to visit Nepal** for trekking, with heavy rain, landslides, and dangerous trails—but a few rain-shadow areas remain trekkable.

![](/images/blog/Misty-terraced-valley-in-green-hues.png)

- **Weather:** Hot, humid, and constantly wet; 80% of annual rainfall occurs during these months.
- **Trekking Conditions:** Muddy, slippery, and treacherous; landslides, flash floods, and leeches are common.
- **Best Treks & Places:** Upper Mustang and Dolpo (rain-shadow areas); Kathmandu Valley for cultural tours.
- **Crowds & Prices:** Minimal crowds and lowest prices—flights, teahouses, and guides are cheap and available.
- **Travel Disruptions:** Domestic flights frequently delayed or cancelled; roads to trailheads can be washed out.

Avoid monsoon for trekking unless heading to rain-shadow regions like Upper Mustang or focusing on cultural tours.

### Winter (December to February)

Winter offers the **clearest mountain views** and quiet trails, but cold temperatures make it best for lower-altitude treks and cultural tours.

![](/images/blog/images%20%284%29.jpg)

- **Weather:** Cold across the country; pleasant days at lower elevations, freezing nights above 3,000m.
- **Mountain Views:** The clearest of the year—crisp, dry air offers uninterrupted Himalayan panoramas.
- **Trekking Conditions:** Good at lower altitudes; high passes may be blocked by snow and many teahouses above 4,000m are closed.
- **Best Treks & Places:** Ghorepani Poon Hill, Mardi Himal, Kathmandu Valley, Pokhara, Chitwan, and Bhaktapur.
- **Crowds & Prices:** Minimal crowds and lowest prices—flights, teahouses, and guides are affordable and easy to book.

Winter is ideal for lower-altitude treks, cultural tours, and travelers seeking solitude and clear mountain views on a budget.

## Is Nepal Safe?

Nepal is generally safe for tourists, but normal precautions apply.

- **Political unrest:** Avoid protests and strikes (_bandhs_). Never participate—it violates visa rules.
- **Petty crime:** Secure valuables, avoid walking alone at night, never leave drinks unattended.
- **Natural disasters:** Earthquakes can occur; monsoon brings landslides and floods.
- **Road safety:** Public buses are risky. Use private transfers or domestic flights.
- **Health:** Medical facilities are limited—get comprehensive travel insurance with evacuation coverage.
- **Altitude sickness:** Real risk above 2,500m. Ascend slowly, hydrate, descend if symptoms worsen.
- **Women travelers:** Generally safe, but dress modestly, avoid isolated areas, trust your instincts.
- **Emergency numbers:** Police 100, Fire 101, Ambulance 102.

With awareness, insurance, and respect for local customs, Nepal is a safe and welcoming destination.

## Nepal Trekking Permits

Most treks in Nepal require at least one permit—some need two or three. Rules have changed significantly in recent years, so it's worth understanding what applies to your specific route before you budget.

### By Region: What You Need

**Everest Region (Everest Base Camp, Gokyo, Three Passes)**

![](/images/blog/Everest-Base-Camp-Header.jpg "Everest Base Camp")

- Sagarmatha National Park Permit — NPR 3,000
- Khumbu Pasang Lhamu Rural Municipality Permit — NPR 2,000–3,000
- TIMS Card — Not required
- Guide — Mandatory

**Annapurna Region (Circuit, Base Camp, Poon Hill, Mardi Himal)**

![](/images/blog/annapurna-poonhill-viewtreks-001.webp "Annapurna Poonhill Trek")

- ACAP Permit — NPR 3,000
- TIMS Card — NPR 1,000–2,000 (enforcement inconsistent—confirm with agency)
- Guide — Mandatory

**Langtang Region (Langtang Valley, Gosainkunda, Helambu)**

![](/images/blog/gosaikunda-lake.webp "Gosainkunda Trek")

- Langtang National Park Permit — NPR 3,000
- TIMS Card — NPR 1,000–2,000
- Guide — Mandatory

**Restricted Areas (Upper Mustang, Manaslu, Dolpo, Kanchenjunga)**

![](/images/blog/manaslu-circuit-trek-all-you-need-to-know1.webp "Manaslu Circuit Trek")

- Restricted Area Permit — USD 50/day (Upper Mustang), USD 70/week (Manaslu, October–November), USD 10/day (other months)
- TIMS Card — Required
- Guide — Mandatory
- Agency Booking — Mandatory

### **Quick Checklist Before You Trek**

- Confirm which permits your route requires
- Book through a registered trekking agency (required for restricted areas)
- Carry passport + 2–4 passport-sized photos
- Arrange proof of travel insurance
- Budget USD 40–70 for standard treks; USD 500+ for restricted areas
- Collect permits from NTB Kathmandu or Pokhara, or at trail checkpoints
- Verify current rules with your agency—they change frequently

### **Good to Know**

- **Guide rule:** Since April 2023, a licensed guide is mandatory on most popular routes. One guide can lead up to seven trekkers.
- **TIMS replaced:** The standard EBC route no longer uses TIMS—it was replaced by the Khumbu Pasang Lhamu permit in 2018.
- **Restricted areas:** As of March 2026, solo trekkers can obtain permits, but a guide is still required.
- **Where to get them:** Nepal Tourism Board offices in Kathmandu (Bhrikutimandap) and Pokhara, or trail entry checkpoints (Monjo for EBC, Syabrubesi for Langtang).

Most treks require a **TIMS card or local permit** plus a **national park entry fee**—typically USD 40–70 total. Restricted areas cost significantly more and require a guide and agency. Rules change often, so always confirm current requirements with your trekking agency before departure. ## Nepal SIM & Internet

![](/images/blog/images%20%286%29.jpg)

**Before You Go**

- Decide: **Ncell** (cities, popular treks, faster) vs **NTC** (remote areas, better above Namche on EBC)
- Consider an **eSIM** (HelloRoam, Ubigi, from \~€3.88) — activate before you fly, no queues
- Download **offline maps** and essential info while you have reliable Wi-Fi

**On Arrival**

- Buy at airport counters, Ncell centres, NTC offices, or dealers near Thamel
- Bring **passport + passport photo**; registration takes \~15 minutes
- Ncell TouristPro: **NPR 595/7 days**, **NPR 995/14 days**, **NPR 1,995/28 days** (unlimited, throttled after 10–25GB)
- NTC: standard prepaid SIM + separate data packs

**On the Trail**

- **Lower villages:** Wi-Fi free or $1–3 per session
- **Higher up:** $3–10 per session, rising with altitude
- **EBC route:** Free Wi-Fi at Lukla and Namche; prepaid cards (NPR 1,200–1,800) above Phakding; **Everest Link** at Dingboche, Lobuche, Gorak Shep; no coverage at Base Camp
- **Manaslu and quieter routes:** Long dead zones — plan accordingly

**Charging**

- Free in lower villages; **$1–5 per charge** at altitude (solar)
- Always carry a **power bank**

Connectivity on the trail is slower, patchier, and pricier than home. Carry a power bank, expect dead zones, and enjoy the disconnect.

## Currency in Nepal

Nepal runs on cash. The official currency is the **Nepali Rupee (NPR)**, and while cards work in cities, you'll need physical rupees for almost everything on the trail. Here's the short version.

**Currency Basics**

- **Nepali Rupee (NPR)**, divided into 100 paisa
- Banknotes: NPR 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 500, 1,000
- Coins: NPR 1, 2, 5, 10 (rarely used on trekking routes)
- **Exchange rate (2026):** roughly **NPR 152 per 1 USD**

**Cash vs Card**

- **Card payments** work in major hotels, upscale restaurants, and tourist shops in Kathmandu and Pokhara
- **Cash is essential** everywhere else—markets, tea houses, transport, and all trekking regions
- **Zero ATMs** on any trekking trail. No tea house accepts cards

**ATMs**

- Reliable in **Kathmandu and Pokhara**, scarce elsewhere
- Per-transaction limit: **NPR 20,000–35,000** (varies by bank)
- ATM fee: **NPR 500** per withdrawal, plus your bank's fee
- Use ATMs **inside bank branches** during opening hours to avoid skimmers

**Money Exchange**

- **Thamel (Kathmandu)** has the highest concentration of licensed money changers and the most competitive rates
- Airport and hotel rates are worse—exchange in the city
- Bring **new, crisp USD bills**; old or torn notes may be rejected

**Denominations for Trekking**

- ATMs dispense **NPR 1,000 notes**, which are hard to break in the mountains
- After withdrawing, visit a bank to get a mix of **NPR 500 and 100 notes**
- Carry plenty of small notes for tea houses, snacks, and tips

**How Much Cash to Carry**

- **Everest Base Camp (14 days):** NPR 70,000–100,000 (USD 500–750)
- **Annapurna Base Camp (10 days):** NPR 50,000–70,000 (USD 370–520)
- Budget travelers can manage on **$15–17/day in cities; mid-range around $27–33/day**

Nepal is a **cash-first economy**. Cards work in cities, but trekking regions are cash-only with no ATMs. Exchange in Thamel, carry small notes, and plan your cash needs before you leave the last town.

## Culture & Etiquette

Nepali culture is warm, welcoming, and deeply rooted in Hindu and Buddhist traditions. A little respect goes a long way—here's what you need to know.

**Greetings**

- Use **"Namaste"** with palms joined together
- Add **"ji"** for formality (e.g., "Namaste, John-ji")
- A **side-to-side head wobble** means "OK" or "I understand"—not "no"

**Temples & Sacred Sites**

![](/images/blog/Pashupatinath-Temple.png "Pashupatinath Temple")

- **Remove shoes** before entering temples, monasteries, and homes
- Walk **clockwise** around temples and stupas
- Some Hindu temples **prohibit non-Hindus**—respect the rules
- Avoid leather items in certain temple areas

**Hands & Feet**

- Use your **right hand** or both hands to give and receive items
- **Never touch anything with your feet** or point feet at people
- Don't step over people or food

**Food & Drink**

- Food touched by another's mouth is **jutho** (impure)—don't share cups or bottles
- Eat with your **right hand** (or utensils)
- Wait to be served or invited before eating

**Photography**

- **Always ask permission** before photographing people
- Some temples and shrines prohibit photography—look for signs
- Respect a "no" without pressure

**Dress Code**

- Dress modestly, especially at religious sites
- Cover shoulders and knees at temples
- Swimwear is only for pools and designated areas

**Public Behavior**

- Avoid **public displays of affection**—considered inappropriate
- Don't point with a single finger; use your open hand
- Keep your voice down in sacred spaces

**Tipping**

- Not mandatory but appreciated
- Trekking guides: $10–15/day
- Porters: $5–10/day
- Restaurants: 10% if service charge isn't included

Respect local customs, dress modestly, use your right hand, and remove shoes at sacred sites. Nepal rewards respectful travelers with warmth and hospitality.

## Travel Responsibly

Nepal's beauty is fragile. Responsible travel ensures local communities benefit, the environment stays clean, and future generations can enjoy the same magic you experience. Here's how to travel with care.

**Support Local**

- Choose **locally owned hotels, teahouses, and homestays** over international chains
- Hire **local guides and porters**—it directly supports mountain communities
- Eat at local restaurants and buy from local markets
- Choose trekking agencies that **pay fair wages** and provide insurance for staff

**Reduce Plastic**

- Carry a **refillable water bottle** with a purification system (tablets, filter, or UV purifier)
- Avoid single-use plastic bottles—Nepal's trails struggle with waste management
- Refuse plastic bags, straws, and cutlery when possible

**Leave No Trace**

- **Pack out what you pack in**—including wrappers, tissues, and batteries
- Stick to **designated trails** to prevent erosion
- Never leave garbage at campsites, teahouses, or viewpoints
- Use **biodegradable soap** and keep it away from water sources

**Respect Wildlife**

- Never feed, touch, or disturb animals
- Keep a safe distance during safaris in Chitwan
- Don't buy products made from endangered species (ivory, fur, shells)

**Conserve Resources**

- Take **short showers**—water is scarce in mountain regions
- Turn off lights and electronics when not in use
- Reuse towels and bedding in hotels
- Carry a **power bank** instead of relying on teahouse charging (often solar-powered)

**Support Community Projects**

- Donate to or volunteer with **local schools, health posts, or environmental groups**
- Buy handicrafts directly from artisans
- Consider **carbon offsetting** your flights

**Be a Conscious Photographer**

- Ask before photographing people, especially in rural areas
- Don't treat people as props—engage with them first
- Share photos respectfully, especially of children

**Choose Responsible Operators**

- Look for agencies committed to **sustainable tourism** and **porter welfare**
- Avoid operators that overload porters or cut corners on safety
- Support companies that give back to local communities

Travel responsibly by supporting locals, reducing plastic, respecting wildlife, and choosing ethical operators. Nepal's mountains and communities will thank you.

## What to Pack for Nepal

Packing for Nepal depends on your itinerary—trekking, cultural tours, or both. Here's a practical breakdown.

**Documents & Essentials**

- Passport (valid 6+ months) + copies
- Visa documents and passport photos
- Travel insurance (with evacuation coverage)
- Cash (USD and NPR) + credit cards
- Passport-sized photos (for permits)
- Printed itinerary and emergency contacts

**Clothing – Layering is Key**

- Moisture-wicking base layers (2–3)
- Fleece or mid-layer
- Down jacket (essential above 3,000m)
- Waterproof/windproof jacket
- Trekking pants (2 pairs)
- Casual clothes for cities
- Warm hat, gloves, buff
- Sunglasses (UV protection)
- Underwear and socks (quick-dry, 4–5 pairs)

**Footwear**

- Broken-in hiking boots (waterproof)
- Camp shoes or sandals
- Gaiters (for snow or mud)

**Trekking Gear**

- Backpack (40–50L) with rain cover
- Duffel bag (for porters)
- Sleeping bag (rated to -10°C for high altitude)
- Trekking poles
- Headlamp + extra batteries
- Water bottle with purifier or tablets
- Snacks (energy bars, nuts)

**Health & Toiletries**

- First aid kit
- Diamox (for altitude sickness—consult doctor)
- Sunscreen (SPF 50+)
- Lip balm with SPF
- Insect repellent
- Hand sanitizer
- Toilet paper and wet wipes
- Personal medications

**Electronics**

- Universal adapter (types C, D, M)
- Power bank
- Charging cables
- Phone with offline maps
- Camera (optional)

**Nice to Have**

- Trekking towel (quick-dry)
- Earplugs and eye mask
- Book or e-reader
- Reusable shopping bag
- Small padlock for luggage
- Altitude sickness medication

Layer clothing, invest in good boots, carry a water purifier, and don't forget Diamox and travel insurance. Pack for the season, but prepare for altitude cold.
