## The Traditional Trekking Calendar: What It Used to Be

For decades, the **best time to visit the Himalayas** followed a predictable rhythm that trekkers, guides, and porters could set their calendars by. At **WaveUp Himalayas**, we grew up with these seasons—they shaped our livelihoods, our celebrations, and the stories we told around fires in teahouses.

**Autumn (September to November)** 

Autumn (September to November) was the crown jewel of **Nepal trekking seasons**. The summer monsoon would retreat by mid-September, leaving behind dry trails, stable weather, and skies so clear you could count the peaks of the **Everest** region from a ridge in Solukhumbu. October was the golden month—families in Kathmandu planned weddings, farmers harvested rice, and trekkers from every corner of the world arrived for the **Everest Base Camp** and **Annapurna** trails. Rainfall data confirmed what we knew instinctively: nearly 80% of Nepal’s annual rain fell during the monsoon, so once it ended, the mountains dried out beautifully.

**Spring (March to May)** 

Spring (March to May) was the second beloved season. Warmer temperatures and longer days made high passes accessible again. The rhododendron forests burst into crimson and pink, and the **Himalayan trekking seasons** offered a different kind of magic—less crowded than autumn, but just as rewarding. Winter’s snow would melt just enough to open routes like the **Annapurna Circuit** and **Everest Three Passes**, though some years remained tricky.

**Winter (December to February)**

 Winter (December to February) was the quiet season. Extreme cold and snow-blocked trails kept most trekkers away, except for the hardy few heading to lower elevations. **Monsoon (June to August)** was the off-season entirely—leeches, landslides, and clouds that swallowed the mountains made it a time for farming, not trekking.

This was the **traditional trekking calendar**—a stable, trustworthy guide that told us when to walk, when to rest, and when to welcome the world to our mountains. But as we now know, that calendar is shifting. The monsoon lasts longer. Snowfall is erratic. Glaciers are melting. And the **best time to trek Nepal** is no longer a fixed date—it’s a moving target. In the next section, we’ll explore exactly how **climate change** is rewriting the rules.

## How Climate Change Is Rewriting the Rules

At **WaveUp Himalayas**, we've watched **climate change** reshape **Nepal's Changing Trekking Seasons** firsthand. The **best time to visit the Himalayas** is no longer predictable.

![](/images/blog/2560px-Glacier_Ngozumpa.jpg)

**The Monsoon Is Lasting Longer**

The monsoon now stretches into October, shrinking the stable autumn window. In 2025, an October blizzard trapped trekkers near **Everest**, and floods killed around 60 people. The **best time to trek Nepal** is shifting later into November.

**Snowfall Is Becoming Erratic**

Warmer air brings less consistent snow but more intense storms. Winter snow arrives late—sometimes February or April—and spring blizzards catch trekkers off guard on high passes like the **Annapurna Circuit**.

**Glaciers Are Melting and Trails Are Changing**

Nearly **15% of glacial ice** has vanished in 50 years. Unstable moraines, increased **rockfall**, **avalanches**, and **GLOF** risks are making trails more dangerous. We've had to reroute treks due to damage that didn't exist before.

**Winter Is Shrinking, But Risks Remain**

Some routes are accessible longer, but melting permafrost and unstable terrain make them riskier than they appear.

**A Crisis Nepal Didn't Create**

**Nepal contributes less than 0.1% of global emissions** and only **0.05% of global CO₂**. Yet we face the worst impacts. Nepal is a green leader: over **90% hydropower electricity**, **70% new cars electric**, and **45% forest cover**. We target **net-zero by 2045**.

**What This Means for Your Trek**

**Spring (March–May)** and **autumn (late September–November)** remain best, but expect variability. October is riskier; spring is less predictable. **WaveUp Himalayas** builds flexible itineraries, prioritizes safety, and offers alternative routes like Upper Mustang.

The **best time to visit the Himalayas** is now a moving target—plan with flexibility and expert local guidance.

## How Nepal Is Adapting

At **WaveUp Himalayas**, we're not just watching **climate change** reshape our mountains—we're part of a national effort to adapt. The **best time to visit the Himalayas** is shifting, and Nepal is responding on multiple fronts.

**Policy-Level Action**

Nepal's **NDC 3.0**, submitted in May 2025, includes adaptation priorities aligned with the National Adaptation Plan. Key measures include **Early Warning Systems**, **Integrated Watershed Management**, and **GLOF risk reduction**—with an estimated **US$18–20 billion** needed for adaptation through 2035. The NDC also commits to maintaining at least **46% forest cover**.

**GLOF Early Warning and Mitigation**

With over **2,000 glacial lakes** in Nepal—21 considered potentially dangerous—the **Green Climate Fund** approved a **$36.1 million** grant in July 2025 to reduce GLOF risks. The seven-year project will benefit around **2.3 million people**, upgrading hazard monitoring and early warning systems, lowering water levels in four high-risk lakes, and restoring over **150 hectares** of land through reforestation.

**Community-Based Ecotourism**

In Sudurpashchim and Karnali Provinces, a new **Climate-Resilient Ecotourism Initiative** is promoting sustainable, community-based tourism and assessing new trekking trails toward **Ramaroshan and Rara Lake**. At the local level, municipalities like **Khumbu Pasang Lhamu** are implementing **Local Adaptation Plans (LAPA)** alongside tourism strategies to build resilience in Everest region communities.

**Tourism Sector Adaptations**

Mountain tourism operators are adopting **reactive and transitional strategies**: extending trekking seasons into traditionally quieter months, selecting **alternative routes** when weather blocks main trails, and promoting **winter expeditions** to cope with unpredictable conditions. In **Sagarmatha National Park**, the 2023 revised Management Plan includes provisions to address overcrowding and regulate tourism activities, though enforcement remains challenging.

**What This Means for Trekkers**

Nepal is adapting—but the **Himalayan trekking seasons** are still shifting. The **best time to trek Nepal** still exists, but it demands flexibility, expert guidance, and respect for the mountains.

## **Is It Safe to Trek in Nepal Now?**

**YES.** With the right preparation, Nepal remains one of the most trekked destinations in the world, and hundreds of thousands of people complete treks here safely every year. The **best time to visit the Himalayas** has become less predictable, but that's a planning challenge—not a safety verdict.

That said, **climate change** has introduced real risks you need to take seriously:

- **Weather is less predictable.** October can now bring snowstorms, and spring can deliver unseasonal blizzards. Landslides and floods during extended monsoons have blocked routes on **Everest** and **Annapurna**, forcing cancellations and costly rescues.
- **Trails are changing.** Melting glaciers are leaving unstable moraine fields and exposed rock. Rockfall and avalanche risks have increased on high passes.
- **Guide requirements are strict.** Since April 2023, a **licensed guide is mandatory** on all major trekking routes in Nepal. The **1:7 rule** means one guide can lead up to seven trekkers. You must also book through a **registered trekking agency** and carry a **TIMS card**.
- **Safety infrastructure is improving.** Nepal's **NepaliPort digital tracking system** helps monitor trekkers and coordinate rescues. **Early Warning Systems** and **GLOF risk reduction** projects are being deployed across high-risk areas.
- **What this means for you:** Trekking in Nepal is safe when you go with a **licensed guide**, choose a reputable agency, build flexibility into your itinerary, and respect the mountains' changing conditions.

- **Build in buffer days.** Weather delays are now common. Add extra days to your itinerary, especially for high passes and domestic flights to Lukla or Jomsom.
- **Choose your season wisely.** **Autumn (late September–November)** and **spring (March–May)** remain the **best time to trek Nepal**, but aim for the heart of these windows—not the edges. November is increasingly reliable for stable autumn weather.
-  **Pack for all seasons.** Even in peak season, you may face heavy rain, deep snow, and intense sun. Layering is essential. Waterproof gear is non-negotiable.
- **Stay informed in real time.** Check local weather forecasts and trail reports before and during your trek. Local guides have the most current information—lean on their expertise.
- **Prioritize acclimatization.** Altitude sickness remains the biggest health risk. Follow your guide's acclimatization schedule, monitor your oxygen levels, and never push through symptoms.

- **Trek with a licensed guide.** It's the law—and it's your best safety asset. Guides are trained in altitude management, emergency coordination, and reading rapidly changing conditions.
